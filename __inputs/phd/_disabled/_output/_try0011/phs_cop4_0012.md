# Personal History Statement – UCSB Computer Science

I grew up in Palo Alto with a childhood immersed in video games. While many of my friends had strict "screen time" limits, my parents—both first-generation college graduates—did something unusual: they encouraged my sister and me to play. Our living room was always set up for LAN parties because to them, games were not a distraction but a place to practice problem solving, persistence, and collaboration. They saw games as educational tools, not temptations to be removed. That early trust in my curiosity shaped how I see technology today: as something to be explored, tinkered with, and used to help people who struggle or are left out.

As I got older, I stopped being satisfied just playing games and started wondering how they worked. I spent afternoons reading wikis about game engines, watching YouTube programming tutorials, and hacking up prototypes in Unity. I joined online game jams where you have a weekend and a theme—"only one button," "everything is a circle"—and have to design something fun, understandable, and playable by strangers. Those jams led naturally to hackathons once I reached college. The format was similar, but the stakes were different: instead of entertaining players, we were building tools under time and resource constraints. Through those experiences I realized that what drew me in was not games alone but computer science more broadly—the craft of making software that solves real problems.

My parents' background made my path into CS easier than it is for many students. They had both put themselves through Canadian universities and wanted my sister and me to focus fully on school. They helped with tuition and living expenses and discouraged us from taking part-time jobs. I have never had to work significant hours or shoulder major family expenses, and I am very aware that this is a privilege.

High school at Henry M. Gunn in Palo Alto added another layer of complexity. Gunn was a high-pressure environment with a strong reputation for academic rigor, but during my time there the community experienced suicide clusters among students. Watching adults stand guard at the Caltrain tracks near campus left a deep impression on me. It made me wary of cultures that celebrate achievement while ignoring mental health. Video games, for me and my classmates, became a low-stakes space to decompress and stay connected. Those experiences are part of why, as a college student, I check in on peers who seem overwhelmed, talk openly about scoping work, and treat sustainable workloads as just as important as grades or publications.

Gunn is one of the most academically competitive public high schools in the country. Many of my classmates had been doing competition math since middle school. I had not. When I struggled with a concept, I assumed the problem was me—that I lacked some essential aptitude that others possessed. I finished high school with a 3.9 GPA, but I did not trust it. When I applied to several UCs, I was admitted only to Santa Cruz, and only off the waitlist. At the time, this felt like confirmation of my doubts.

I carried that uncertainty into my undergraduate years. I worked hard, but I hedged. After graduating, I chose a Master's degree at UCSB rather than a PhD because I was afraid to commit. I needed to know if I could handle the rigor. Looking back, I see a pattern: I notice gaps—between my confidence and my ability, between who I thought I was and what I could actually do—and I slowly close them.

I completed my B.S. at UC Santa Cruz and my M.S. at UC Santa Barbara in a combined three and a half years. At UCSC I met classmates whose high schools did not offer the same range of AP or honors courses that mine did. They were motivated, but when they reached core CS courses, they faced material my own high school had drilled for years. Some failed classes more than once and left the major entirely. Having grown up in a high-income town with time to focus on academics, I do not treat GPAs as neutral indicators of talent. Seeing how uneven preparation can be made me feel responsible for using my training to build tools that support those whose paths have been steeper than mine.

This awareness shapes the projects I choose. For Dr. Misha Sra's class, I prototyped GUARD, a phone-based assistant that cross-checks documents and listens during high-stakes conversations—such as a landlord explaining a lease or a doctor making recommendations—and uses AI to highlight likely problems and suggest follow-up questions. The idea came from watching my non-native-speaking relatives struggle with healthcare instructions and sign contracts they did not fully understand. In those situations, the rules of the game are opaque to anyone without medical or legal fluency. GUARD offers another chance to notice problems and ask questions before committing to terms.

In another project, SymbolSight, I worked on visual symbol sets designed to remain distinguishable despite the distortions caused by retinal implants. The goal was to help people who have lost their vision regain reading ability—not by changing the hardware, but by choosing symbols that stay clear under prosthetic limitations. The project was voted the top project in the class, and I am now preparing a paper for IEEE EMBC 2026 with Dr. Michael Beyeler.

Mentorship has become the way I try to pass forward what I have received. For two years, I have given guest lectures in Dr. Daniel Shapiro's Game AI class at UCSC, talking with undergraduates about how to scope feasible projects, navigate AI tools, and submit research papers. Many of them remind me of myself—curious but uncertain, interested but unsure if they belong. I try to be the person I needed: someone who says, concretely, here is how you do this, and here is why you can.

Outside of academics, I volunteer weekly at Silicon Valley Bicycle Exchange, a community shop that repairs donated bikes for people who need transportation to work or school. When I started, I could barely fix my own flat tire. Now I help fill trucks with kids' and commuter bikes I helped restore. The riders will not know my name, but they will feel working brakes and wheels that do not rub.

These are small actions. I do not want to overstate them. But they reflect something I believe: that access to knowledge and encouragement is unevenly distributed, and that those of us who have received it have some responsibility to pass it on. If I am admitted, I will be the first person in my family to earn a PhD. I will bring experience building interactive systems for children and non-experts, awareness of the structural advantages that shaped my own education, and a commitment to projects that support people left out of standard designs. I am ready to move from questioning my place in this field to leading within it.
