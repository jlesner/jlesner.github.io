Our living room in Palo Alto was arranged less like a formal sitting area and more like a tiny LAN café. The coffee table was pushed aside to make room for laptops and controllers, and my parents didn’t scold my sister and me for “too much screen time.” Instead, they asked what we were playing and what we were learning. For them—both first-generation college graduates who had put themselves through Canadian universities—games were practice grounds for problem solving, persistence, and collaboration, not distractions to be eliminated. Growing up in that space, I learned to see technology as something to explore, tinker with, and eventually bend toward helping people who are struggling or left out. 

By middle school I was no longer satisfied just playing games. I wanted to know how they worked. I read game engine wikis after homework, followed YouTube tutorials, and stitched together clumsy prototypes in Unity. Online game jams became my weekend routine: you have 48 hours, a theme like “only one button,” and strangers on the internet waiting to play. Later, college hackathons felt like the same format with different stakes. Instead of entertaining players, we were racing to build tools that solved real problems under tight constraints. That was when it clicked that what I loved was not only games, but computer science as a craft for making interactive systems that meet people where they are.

My parents’ support removed barriers that many of my peers faced. They covered tuition and living expenses and discouraged me from taking on part-time work, urging me to focus on learning. I have never had to send money home, work long hours in addition to school, or pause my education to handle family crises. I am acutely aware that this is a significant advantage, and it shapes how I think about fairness in education and in computing.

High school at Henry M. Gunn in Palo Alto complicated that story. Gunn is known for its academic rigor and for the intense pressure many students feel. During my time there, the community experienced suicide clusters. I remember seeing adults stationed near the Caltrain tracks, there not for traffic but to watch over students. That image stayed with me. It made me wary of cultures that celebrate achievement while quietly burning people out. For many of us, video games became a low-stakes way to decompress and stay connected—logging on for a quick co-op session or just talking over a casual match when everything else felt high stakes. 

Academically, I did well on paper, finishing with around a 3.9 GPA. But surrounded by classmates who had been doing competition math for years, I internalized a quieter story: that I was “behind,” that whatever spark serious researchers had, I did not. When I applied to the UC system, I was rejected by most campuses and admitted to UC Santa Cruz only off the waitlist. It felt like confirmation that I was not the kind of person who goes on to do a PhD. That belief followed me into college for far longer than my transcript would suggest.

At UC Santa Cruz and later UC Santa Barbara, I completed my B.S. and M.S. in a combined three and a half years, a pace made possible by not needing outside work. In my CS classes, I met classmates whose high schools had offered only a fraction of the advanced courses mine did. They were just as motivated, but when they hit data structures or algorithms, they were encountering concepts my own school had drilled years earlier. Some failed core courses multiple times or left the major. Seeing this made it impossible for me to treat GPAs or “rigor” as neutral measures of merit. It pushed me to look for ways my technical work could support people whose starting points were very different from mine. 

That instinct—to notice gaps in access or understanding and then build tools around them—shows up across my projects. One example is my AI Personalized Interactive Fiction (AIPIF) project, which started as a course assignment in UCSC’s Game AI class. I set out to design story-based games for young children that adapt to their choices and interests in real time, using language models not just as black boxes but as collaborators constrained by careful scaffolding. That class project grew into a paper and demo presented at PAIS-24 and ECAI-24. Now, my advisor Dr. Shapiro invites me back each year to guest lecture in that same Game AI course. Instead of presenting myself as a prodigy, I walk students through the messy middle—scope creep, broken builds, rejected drafts—and share concrete strategies for working with LLMs, choosing complementary teammates, and avoiding the unhealthy “crunch” cycles that academic culture sometimes normalizes. For many of them, just hearing “yes, you can do this too” matters as much as any technical advice. 

In Dr. Misha Sra’s course on interactive and real-time user experiences of AI, I shifted that same mindset toward people facing high-stakes decisions with limited information. I designed GUARD, a phone-based assistant that cross-checks written and spoken communication—leases, medical instructions, emails—and flags ambiguities, discrepancies, or potential risks. The idea came from watching non-native English speakers in my extended family sign documents they did not fully understand or leave medical appointments unsure if they had missed something important. GUARD uses progressive disclosure: an overview tab for quick risk summaries, deeper views for evidence and suggested questions, and clear confidence indicators so users can decide how much to trust the system. In a small user study, we saw that people evaluating repetitive tasks (like e-commerce moderation) preferred standardized, almost checklist-style explanations, while those in one-off, personal situations wanted adaptive explanations and strong privacy controls. Designing for that difference taught me how context shapes trust, and how easily “helpful” AI can become overwhelming or opaque if explanation design is an afterthought. 

At SBHacks 2025, I helped build SnipDue, a web app that lets students paste messy course schedules and instantly turn them into editable calendar events. We used Claude 3.5 Sonnet, running on Cloudflare Workers, to interpret varied syllabus formats, and built an interactive drag-and-drop interface so students could correct the AI before syncing deadlines to their calendars. The point was not to automate students out of the loop, but to let the model do the tedious parsing while humans kept final control. SnipDue ended up winning “Best Use of Gen AI” and was later presented as a poster at UCSB’s AI CoP Spring Symposium, but what mattered most to me was feedback from students who worked jobs or had caregiving responsibilities. For them, shaving an hour off the start of each quarter and avoiding missed deadlines is not a convenience—it is a way to make overloaded lives slightly more manageable. 

In CMPSC 291A: Bionic Vision with Dr. Michael Beyeler, I worked on SymbolSight, a project that asked a different kind of access question: can we design a set of visual symbols that remain legible even after the distortions introduced by retinal implants? Instead of treating implant hardware as fixed and alphabet glyphs as inevitable, we constructed a pipeline that generated candidate symbols from diverse sources (like DCT patterns, Katakana, and Braille), simulated prosthetic distortions using pulse2percept, estimated confusion rates with neural networks, and optimized letter-to-symbol mappings using language statistics. Our simulations suggested that carefully chosen symbol sets can significantly outperform standard letters under high distortion, and the project was voted the top project in the class. We are now preparing a paper for submission to IEEE EMBC. This work reminded me that “accessibility” is not a cosmetic add-on; it often requires rethinking defaults at a very deep level. 

Mentorship and community work are where I try to turn these insights into ongoing practice. Beyond guest lecturing in Game AI, I make time to talk with undergraduates who feel like I once did: interested in research but convinced they are not “that type of person.” I share concrete steps—how to email a professor, how to scope a first project, how to recover when experiments fail—and I am honest about my own doubts. The goal is to lower the invisible barriers, not by promising that the path is easy, but by making it visible and navigable.

Outside academia, I volunteer at Silicon Valley Bicycle Exchange, a community bike shop that repairs donated bikes for people who need reliable transportation. When I started, I barely knew how to fix a flat. Now I can help tune brakes, true wheels, and sign off on safety checks before bikes are loaded into trucks and delivered. The riders will never know who worked on their frames, but they will feel the difference in a bike that just works. That quiet, behind-the-scenes impact feels similar to good interface design or infrastructure in AI systems: when it’s done well, most people never notice it directly, but it expands what they can do.

I do not fit traditional markers of disadvantage. I come from a stable, supportive family, and I’ve had the freedom to focus on school and research. At the same time, years in competitive academic environments left me with deep imposter feelings that shaped how I move through the field. Those experiences have made me more attentive to who disappears from our classrooms, whose needs are not considered in our systems, and how psychological safety intersects with technical rigor.

As a PhD student in Computer Science at UC Santa Barbara, I hope to keep building human-centered AI systems that make complex processes more transparent and fair: tools that help students manage overwhelming workloads, non-experts question opaque recommendations, and people with sensory impairments access information on their own terms. I also hope to contribute to a graduate culture that values sustainable work habits and honest mentorship as much as it values publications. I am ready to move from questioning my place in this field to taking responsibility within it—using the advantages I have had, and the doubts I have worked through, to support others as we build more equitable and trustworthy AI together.
