Personal History Statement
University of California, Santa Barbara

My hands were covered in grease, and I was struggling with a rusted brake cable. It was a Saturday morning at the Silicon Valley Bicycle Exchange, a community non-profit where volunteers repair donated bikes for people who need them to get to work or school. I had started volunteering there a few months prior, unable to fix a flat tire on my own bike. Slowly, I learned. I learned how to true a wheel, how to adjust a derailleur, and how to tell when a frame was too bent to save.

There is a specific quiet satisfaction in that work. The people who eventually ride these bikes will never know my name. They won’t know I spent forty minutes scrubbing the chain. They will just know that when they squeeze the brakes, the bike stops. That when they pedal, the gears shift without grinding.

I look for this kind of work—technical, behind-the-scenes, and focused on lowering barriers for others—because for a long time, I didn't think I belonged in the room where the engineering happened.

I grew up in Palo Alto, a place that projects an image of effortless brilliance. By high school, it felt like everyone around me already had a roadmap to Stanford, a research internship, and a startup idea. I did not. At Gunn High School, the academic pressure was a physical weight in the air. During my time there, the community experienced suicide clusters among students. I remember seeing adults standing guard at the Caltrain tracks near campus, ensuring no one jumped. It left a deep impression on me. It made me wary of cultures that celebrate achievement while ignoring the human cost.

While my peers seemed to breezily grasp advanced calculus, I struggled. I internalized a narrative that I was behind, that I lacked the innate "spark" required for deep technical work. I finished high school with a 3.9 GPA, but I didn't trust it. When I applied to colleges, I was rejected by most of the UC system and admitted to UC Santa Cruz only off the waitlist. I carried that sense of imposter syndrome with me for years.

My refuge was the living room. My parents, both first-generation college graduates, did something unusual for Palo Alto parents: they didn't limit screen time. They encouraged my sister and me to play video games. To them, games were not a distraction; they were a gymnasium for problem-solving and collaboration. In games, failure wasn't a judgment on my intelligence; it was just data. It was part of the loop. That early trust shaped how I see technology today: not as a test of worth, but as a tool to be explored and tinkered with.

However, I also know my path was cleared by privilege. My parents put themselves through Canadian universities and wanted us to focus fully on school. They paid my tuition and living expenses. I never had to work a night shift to make rent or skip class to pick up a sibling. I completed my B.S. and M.S. in a combined three and a half years, a speed made possible only because I had no other burdens.

At UCSC, I met classmates who were just as curious as I was but had attended high schools that didn't offer AP Computer Science. They faced material I had been exposed to for years, but they were seeing it for the first time while working part-time jobs. Some failed core classes. Some left the major. Watching this inequality play out made me realize that "merit" in computer science is often just a proxy for preparation and resources.

This realization changed how I chose my projects. I stopped caring about building the flashiest tech and started looking for places where systems were opaque or unfair.

During my Master’s at UCSB, I took a course with Dr. Misha Sra on Interactive AI. I began thinking about my non-native English-speaking relatives. I had watched them struggle to understand healthcare instructions or sign contracts they didn't fully grasp. The "rules of the game" were hidden from them.

I didn't want to just pity them; I wanted to build a tool. I prototyped GUARD (Guided Understanding & Agreement Rights Detector). It was a phone-based assistant that listened to high-stakes conversations—like a landlord explaining a lease—and cross-checked documents to highlight inconsistencies. I had to figure out how to present complex AI judgments on a small screen without overwhelming a user who was already stressed. I ran a user study comparing different explanation strategies. The technical challenge was fascinating, but the motivation was personal: giving agency back to people who usually have to trust blindly.

I found a similar drive in Dr. Michael Beyeler’s Bionic Vision course. We were studying retinal implants, which restore limited vision to the blind. The hardware is incredibly constrained, often producing distorted, noisy percepts. The standard approach is to try to make the hardware better. My project, SymbolSight, asked a different question: instead of fixing the eye, can we fix the input?

I ran simulations to find symbols—borrowing from Braille, Katakana, and icons—that remained distinct even when distorted by the implant’s limitations. It was a messy, iterative process of running neural network proxies for human perception, tweaking parameters, and trying again. But the result was a system that could potentially help someone read again. It was the same feeling as fixing the brakes in the bike shop: tinkering with the mechanics to ensure someone else has a smooth ride.

Now, I try to pass that perspective on. For the past two years, I have guest lectured in Dr. Daniel Shapiro’s Game AI class. I talk about a project I built, AIPIF, which started as a class assignment and became a published paper.

When I lecture, I don’t present myself as a prodigy. I tell them about the rejected drafts. I talk about the "messy middle" where nothing works. I look at the students who sit in the back, the ones who look unsure if they belong, and I try to be the person I needed at their age. I help them scope projects that are feasible so they don't burn out. I talk openly about mental health and avoiding "crunch" culture. I want them to know that you don't have to be a genius to do this work; you just have to be persistent, and you have to care.

I am applying to the PhD program at UCSB because I am done hedging. After my undergraduate degree, I chose a Master’s because I was afraid to commit to a PhD. I was afraid I would fail. But through my research with Dr. Sra and Dr. Beyeler, and my work in the community, I have learned to trust my own hands.

I bring a specific perspective to this department. I bring the resilience of someone who had to fight off deep imposter syndrome, and the ethical weight of someone who knows their presence here is partly due to luck and privilege. I want to continue building human-centered AI tools that support people left out of standard designs—whether due to disability, language barriers, or lack of resources.

I am ready to do the work. I am ready to fix the brakes, write the code, and hold the door open for the students coming in behind me.
