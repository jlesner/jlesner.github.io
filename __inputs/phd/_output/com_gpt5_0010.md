## Statement of Purpose – UCSB Computer Science PhD

The first time I saw my system turn an “unsupported” SQL pair into a cleanly “refuted” one, it was close to midnight in the UCSB lab. The SMT verifier had just timed out on yet another tricky query, and I was watching my logs scroll by: LLM rewrite, verifier call, database check, repeat. When the pipeline finally produced a counterexample that survived all three stages—LLM, formal logic, and actual database execution—it felt like a small but concrete proof that we can make AI-driven tools behave in ways we can trust.

That moment captures the core question that motivates my PhD plans: how can we combine powerful but opaque AI systems with structure, verification, and optimization so that their behavior stays reliable even in messy, real-world settings? I am applying to the UCSB Computer Science PhD program to pursue that question through research on large language models (LLMs), automatic prompt optimization, and hybrid systems that connect AI to formal methods and databases. My long-term goal is to lead research in industry or an applied lab, building AI systems that are not only accurate on benchmarks but dependable enough for safety-critical domains.

My path to this point has been shaped by equal parts curiosity and hesitation. At Palo Alto’s Gunn High School, surrounded by classmates headed to top universities, I struggled with math and quietly assumed that “real research” was for other people. Even after earning a 3.9 GPA in my BSc, I was admitted only to UCSC and initially chose a master’s over a PhD because I doubted that I belonged on such a long research path. Completing my one-year CS master’s at UCSB changed that. Graduate-level courses in machine learning, databases, and AI systems gave me the theoretical grounding I had been missing, and my projects there convinced me that I can not only understand complex systems but also push them in new directions.

My first serious research experience came as an undergraduate with **State Machine Visualizer (SMV)**, a tool for automatically extracting and visualizing state machines from students’ mechatronics code. The research problem sounded simple—turn messy C source into accurate diagrams—but quickly became an exercise in program analysis and tool design. I built a static analysis pipeline that parsed C into an abstract syntax tree using PycParser, then used XPATH and XSLT to identify states, transitions, and guards before rendering them with GraphViz. I tested SMV on two years of student projects, iteratively hardening it so it could handle wildly different coding styles. Even after the initial CAHSI funding ended, I kept working on SMV because I wanted it to be robust enough for other instructors to use. That experience taught me that I enjoy the slow, iterative process of turning messy reality into reliable tools.

During the gap between my BSc and my master’s, I chose to keep doing research on my own. In **AI Personalized Interactive Fiction (AIPIF)**, a project that later became a full paper at PAIS 2024 and a demo at ECAI 2024, I built a system that lets children co-create branching stories with generative models while parents review and approve the content. Technically, this meant designing a “tree of thought” pipeline where LLMs authored parallel story branches, orchestrated by a web service and GPU worker pool that handled text, images, sound, and music. I learned how to constrain generative models to strict formats, deal with context limits, and orchestrate multiple AI components into a single coherent application. More importantly, I realized how much I enjoy building systems that are judged not by toy examples but by whether families can actually use them safely.

At UCSB, my research has moved closer to the foundations of reliability. In **DBDoctor**, I designed a hybrid verification system that combines LLM-guided rewriting with an SMT-based SQL verifier. Many real-world SQL equivalence questions fall outside the verifier’s supported fragment, especially queries involving window functions or tricky list semantics. In DBDoctor, I built an “LLM refutation-by-rewrite loop” where a model rewrites unsupported query pairs into forms the verifier can handle, then uses database execution to validate any counterexamples the verifier finds. I evaluated this on nearly 24,000 LeetCode query pairs and showed that the system could shrink the fraction of “unsupported” pairs to roughly 1% while discovering many new counterexamples missed under practical time budgets. Designing DBDoctor taught me how to use LLMs not as oracles but as fallible components inside a disciplined loop that includes formal reasoning and empirical checks.

My master’s project, **HAPO: Hyper-Reflection for Automatic Prompt Optimization**, pushed that idea to the level of prompts and model behavior themselves. Building on the GEPA (Genetic-Pareto) framework, I used frontier models (Claude Opus, GPT-5, Gemini 2.5) to optimize the reflection instructions that guide a compact model (Qwen3-8B) on two SQL verification tasks: example generation and counterexample discovery. I implemented a full APO pipeline in DSPy, serving models with vLLM and extending context windows from 8k to over 40k tokens using YaRN to accommodate more complex reasoning traces. Along the way, I discovered a “thinking truncation” failure mode where the optimized prompts caused the compact model to spend its entire budget on chain-of-thought reasoning without leaving tokens for an answer. Diagnosing and fixing that bug—by adjusting prompts, budgets, and context—drove home a lesson that now shapes my research interests: context is compute, and sophisticated reasoning strategies must be matched to the resources and failure modes of the models that use them.

These projects have pointed me toward a research agenda centered on **inference-time LLM scaling, automatic prompt optimization, and hybrid AI-formal systems**. I am particularly interested in:

* Designing prompt optimizers that can adapt to model updates and distribution shifts without retraining weights
* Leveraging trajectory logs from optimizers like GEPA to train lightweight prompt-rewriting policies (“LEAP – Learning from Evolved and Augmented Prompts”)
* Integrating these learned controllers into larger systems like DBDoctor, where prompts, verifiers, and databases interact to produce trustworthy answers.

UCSB is the place where I want to pursue this work. I have already spent a year in the department, working closely with **Professor Xifeng Yan** on HAPO and DBDoctor and learning the culture of his lab. His research on foundation models, multi-agent systems, and DSLs like ADL/MICA—where agents and their prompts are specified declaratively and executed by a multi-agent engine—aligns directly with my interest in prompt-level controllers and inference-time optimization. I see clear opportunities to map LEAP-style prompt learners onto ADL/MICA’s agent prompts and flows, replacing hand-tuned YAML with prompts distilled from optimization trajectories. I am also excited about continuing to collaborate with **Distinguished Professor Amr El Abbadi** and **Dr. Fuheng Zhao** on intersections of LLMs, databases, and verification.

In the long term, I want to lead research that brings robust AI into high-stakes domains such as oncology and scientific discovery. My strengths are in software, optimization, and system design, and I believe I can have the most impact by building the foundations—prompt optimizers, verification loops, and hybrid architectures—that make these applications safe to deploy. A UCSB CS PhD would give me the depth, mentorship, and collaborative environment to do that work well. I already know what it feels like to belong to this community, and I am eager to return not just as a student but as a researcher who helps shape its next generation of trustworthy AI systems.

## Personal History Statement – UCSB Computer Science

On my uncle’s fridge there used to be a row of appointment cards, each one a small white rectangle with a date, a time, and the word “Oncology.” As a teenager, I would stand in his kitchen and trace that row with my eyes, counting how many cards were left and wondering which one might be the last. I didn’t have the language for it then, but I was already thinking about uncertainty, risk, and what it means to trust systems that make life-changing decisions.

Cancer has shaped my family’s story. Both my grandfather and my uncle died from it, and as a blood relative I live with the knowledge that my mother, my sister, myself, and any future children may face the same risk. That background does not make me a biologist or clinician, but it colors how I see technology. When I hear about an AI system used in screening or treatment, I don’t picture a generic user; I picture my family in that kitchen, reading appointment cards and trying to make sense of probabilities. It’s one reason I care so much about reliability and transparency in AI, even when my current projects sit on the “infrastructure” side of things.

At the same time, I grew up in Palo Alto and attended Gunn High School, a place where it sometimes felt like everyone else already had a roadmap to Stanford, research internships, and startup ideas. I did well academically, but I internalized a quiet assumption that I wasn’t the kind of person who goes on to do a PhD. No one in my immediate family worked in academia or tech; the people I knew who did cutting-edge work were always someone else’s parents. That mix—family health worries on one side, and a high-pressure academic environment on the other—left me with a strange combination of urgency and self-doubt.

When I arrived at UC Santa Cruz, I finally found a space where curiosity mattered more than pedigree. My first real step into research, the State Machine Visualizer project, started as a funded opportunity through the CAHSI program and the ECE118 mechatronics course, but it became much more personal when the funding ended. I kept working on the tool, refining its static analysis pipeline and testing it on years of student code, because I could see how it made life easier for students who were just trying to get their robots to move. It felt important that someone make debugging tools that didn’t assume you already spoke the “right” technical dialect. In hindsight, that was my first small act of inclusion in CS: building infrastructure to help students who were overwhelmed by the same complexity that had intimidated me.

The theme of translating complex systems into accessible experiences became clearer in **AI Personalized Interactive Fiction (AIPIF)**. In that project, I built a system that lets young children craft branching stories with generative models, while giving parents a review console where they can approve or regenerate content before it reaches the child. On the surface, it was a project about interactive fiction and multimodal AI. Underneath, it was about control and trust: how to let children enjoy powerful tools without exposing them to unsafe or confusing content, and how to give parents enough visibility that they feel comfortable saying yes. Designing parental oversight mechanisms—media galleries, regenerate buttons, and a clear audit trail—forced me to think about users who are often left out of AI conversations: kids, non-technical caregivers, and families with limited time or resources to supervise technology.

Those concerns carried into how I navigate my own opportunities. During my UCSB master’s, I made the difficult decision to withdraw from three graduate courses so I could travel to Europe to present AIPIF at PAIS 2024 and ECAI 2024. On paper, it looks like a strategic research move. In reality, it was a stressful period of visa paperwork, financial planning, and late-night doubts about whether I was throwing away my academic standing for a conference trip that might not lead anywhere. I leaned on mentors, asked uncomfortable questions about how to come back and finish my degree, and accepted that I would have to rebuild my course plan afterwards. Standing in front of my poster in Europe, speaking too fast and forgetting to ask people to vote for my demo, I realized that I still had a lot to learn about conferences—but I also realized that I could survive stretching beyond what felt “safe.” That experience has made me more empathetic toward students who are navigating similar tradeoffs without a clear script.

Mentorship and community have become the ways I try to pass that empathy on. For the past two years, I have given guest lectures in Dr. Shapiro’s Game AI class, talking candidly about how a course project turned into AIPIF and how I navigated the logistics of writing and submitting papers. I don’t present myself as a prodigy; instead, I walk through the messy intermediate steps: the failed prototypes, the drafts that didn’t make sense, the emails I was afraid to send. Students—especially those from underrepresented backgrounds—have told me that seeing a path from “just a class project” to a conference publication makes research feel more reachable.

At UCSC and UCSB, I have also spent time around the Women in Computer Science (WiCS) community, informally mentoring undergraduates who are curious about LLMs and research. Often these conversations are not about equations or code but about confidence: how to approach a professor after class, how to handle being one of the few women in a systems course, how to recover from a bad midterm. I don’t pretend to have all the answers, but I do try to be honest about my own doubts and the strategies I’ve used to keep going. In that sense, my contribution to diversity is less about any single initiative and more about showing up consistently as someone who remembers what it feels like to think you don’t “look like” a researcher.

My personal history also shapes the questions I want to pursue with a UCSB CS PhD. Growing up with cancer in my family has made health and risk impossible to ignore, so when I think about trustworthy AI, I don’t stop at databases or benchmarks. I imagine eventual applications to oncology: systems that help clinicians detect patterns earlier, simulate treatment options more safely, or communicate uncertainty more clearly to patients and families. I know that getting there will require working in interdisciplinary teams and listening closely to people whose expertise is very different from mine. The habits I’ve developed—building tools for students, designing parental oversight interfaces, and mentoring undergraduates as they navigate unfamiliar territory—are all practice for that kind of collaborative, patient-centered work.

At UCSB, I hope to contribute to the community in at least three ways. First, by continuing to mentor and guest-teach, especially for students who are new to research or come from backgrounds where PhDs are rare. Second, by helping build open-source tools—whether debugging aids, educational platforms, or prompt-optimization frameworks—that lower the barrier to working with advanced AI. And third, by participating in and helping organize spaces like WiCS-adjacent reading groups or outreach events that connect UCSB to local schools, so that students who remind me of my younger self can see a concrete path into computing.

I do not fit every traditional marker of “disadvantage,” but my experiences with family illness, self-doubt in competitive academic environments, and the often invisible labor of mentoring have shaped how I move through computer science. They have taught me to pay attention to who is left out of our systems and conversations, and to see my technical work as one part of a broader responsibility to make AI safer and more accessible. As a PhD student at UCSB, I want to keep doing that work—turning my personal history into steady, practical contributions to a graduate community that is more diverse, supportive, and prepared to build technology worthy of the trust people place in it.
