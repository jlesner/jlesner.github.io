The logs on my screen were almost empty. A compact language model had spent its entire generation budget “thinking” about a SQL query and produced no answer at all. It was past midnight, and I was deep in my master’s project on Hyper-Reflection for Automatic Prompt Optimization. Staring at that useless trace, I realized the failure wasn’t about creativity. It was about reliability.

Until then, I had thought of research as invention from a blank slate: new models, new features, new tricks. That night forced me to ask a different question: what does it take to make AI systems behave predictably when they are embedded in messy, real tasks and finite compute budgets? Since then, my work has focused on trustworthy AI, especially inference-time scaling for large language models and their intersection with formal verification.

As an undergraduate in computer science at UC Santa Cruz, I joined an NSF-funded REU with Professor Gabriel Elkaim and began building the State Machine Visualizer, a tool to automatically generate diagrams of autopilot state machines from students’ C code. The idea sounded straightforward; in practice, students’ wildly different coding styles broke every brittle pattern I tried.

My first prototype, which depended on heuristics over raw source, collapsed on realistic inputs. I restarted it using Abstract Syntax Tree analysis and rule-based extraction of states and transitions, then spent several quarters hardening the system so it could handle a wide range of lab submissions while still producing diagrams instructors trusted. Today, State Machine Visualizer is used in UCSC’s embedded systems labs, and I am preparing a paper about it. That experience taught me that I enjoy the slow, iterative process of turning messy reality into reliable tools.

My next project pushed me to think about safety in a more explicitly human context. In a Game AI course, I started what became **AIPIF** (AI Personalized Interactive Fiction), a generative storytelling tool for children. I wanted kids to choose characters and goals while parents retained control over tone and content. I designed a simple pipeline where the model filled in constrained templates, and parents reviewed each page of the story before the child saw it. Supported by CAHSI funding, this work became a peer-reviewed paper and an international demo. AIPIF convinced me that reliability is also socio-technical: it depends on workflows and guardrails, not just algorithms.

At UCSB, working with Professor Xifeng Yan has allowed me to connect these intuitions to formal methods and large-scale systems. In his foundational models course, I helped start **DBDoctor**, a system that uses large language models to extend the coverage of SMT-based SQL equivalence verifiers. Existing verifiers struggle with modern SQL features like window functions and nested queries. Rather than rebuilding the verifier, we used LLMs to propose candidate rewrites and counterexamples, while the SMT solver remained the final authority on correctness. We designed the system so that model errors create extra work—more candidates to check—rather than incorrect claims about equivalence.

After DBDoctor, Professor Yan encouraged me to focus my master’s research on automatic prompt optimization, culminating in my UCSB Master project **HAPO (Hyper-Reflection for Automatic Prompt Optimization)**. Using the GEPA framework, I treat prompts as search objects rather than hand-crafted artifacts, design verifiable tasks such as SQL synthesis and counterexample discovery, and let smaller “worker” models attempt them while a stronger “reflector” proposes refinements. In early experiments, naïve chain-of-thought prompting often pushed models into what I call a “thinking truncation” failure mode: they used the entire context window explaining their reasoning and never produced a final answer. By explicitly budgeting context and training the system to recognize when to stop thinking, I improved success rates while keeping token costs under control. This led me to a guiding principle I call “context is compute”: inference-time resources must be managed as carefully as parameters during training.

These projects shape the research questions I now want to pursue in a PhD. How can we design inference-time algorithms—prompt optimizers, tool-using agents, verifier-in-the-loop systems—that come with meaningful guarantees about failure modes? How can we combine symbolic structure, such as program semantics, with LLM flexibility so that model mistakes surface as extra work instead of harmful actions? I am especially interested in applying these ideas to domains like oncology, where my own family has been deeply affected by cancer, and where clinicians need AI they can safely ignore when it is unsure and trust when it acts.

I am applying to the PhD program in Computer Science at UC Santa Barbara because it is the best place for me to continue this line of work. My master’s research with Professor Yan already points directly toward the questions I want to answer: scalable prompt optimization, reliable inference-time reasoning, and LLM-driven tools that can be trusted in high-stakes settings. His group’s focus on practical, robust systems—ranging from large-scale data mining to LLM adapters and multi-agent systems—aligns closely with my interests.

I am choosing a PhD rather than going directly to industry because I want the time and mentorship to push beyond one-off prototypes. The work that has motivated me most—State Machine Visualizer, AIPIF, DBDoctor, and HAPO—involved long arcs of design, failure, revision. A PhD will let me deepen my understanding of formal verification and optimization while continuing to build systems that real users rely on.

After my PhD, I hope to work in industry research, building AI systems for healthcare and other high-stakes domains. My goal is to lead work on trustworthy AI that stays grounded in real workflows. In the long term, I want to help develop inference-time methods that make AI systems robust to distribution shift, transparent about their reasoning, and accountable to the people they serve.

A PhD at UCSB is a necessary next step toward that goal. I have already begun this trajectory through my master’s work, publications, and international conference presentations, and I am eager to keep going. I am ready to do the slow, careful work of building AI systems that are not only powerful, but dependable enough for the real world.
