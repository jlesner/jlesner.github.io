Personal History Statement

I grew up in Palo Alto, a city where the future is often written in code. While many of my friends had strict “screen time” limits, my parents—both first-generation college graduates—did something unusual: they encouraged my sister and me to play video games. Our living room was frequently set up for “LAN parties,” cables snaking across the floor, connecting eager friends to shared digital worlds. To my parents, games were not a distraction to be minimized but a sandbox for problem-solving, persistence, and collaboration. They saw them as educational tools rather than temptations. That early trust in my curiosity shaped how I see technology today: not just as a product to be consumed, but as a medium to be explored, tinkered with, and ultimately used to help people who are otherwise left out.

As I got older, I stopped being satisfied with just playing. I wanted to know how the systems worked. I spent afternoons reading wikis about game engines, watching programming tutorials on YouTube, and hacking together prototypes in Unity. I joined online game jams, where the constraints—themes like “only one button” or “everything is a circle”—forced me to design things that were understandable and playable by strangers in a single weekend. These jams led naturally to hackathons in college. The stakes changed, but the core appeal remained: building tools under constraint to solve a specific problem. Through these experiences, I realized that what drew me in was not the games themselves, but the broader craft of computer science—the ability to build systems that people could use.

However, my path to this realization was not a straight line. Growing up, I attended Henry M. Gunn High School, a place known for intense academic pressure. While my peers seemed to effortlessly grasp advanced calculus, I often struggled. I internalized a narrative that I was behind, that I lacked the innate “spark” required for deep technical work. During my time there, the community experienced suicide clusters among students. Watching adults stand guard at the Caltrain tracks near campus left a deep impression on me. It made me wary of cultures that celebrate achievement while ignoring the human cost. Video games became a low-stakes space to decompress and stay connected. Those experiences are why, as a college student, I prioritize sustainable workloads and check in on peers who seem overwhelmed. I learned early that grades are not the only metric that matters.

When I applied to colleges, I was rejected by most of the UC system and admitted to UC Santa Cruz only off the waitlist. I carried that sense of imposter syndrome with me for years. I finished high school with a 3.9 GPA, but I didn’t trust it. I assumed my struggle with certain concepts meant I wasn't "cut out" for this field.

Yet, I also had a massive advantage. My parents, having put themselves through Canadian universities, wanted my sister and me to focus fully on school. They covered tuition and living expenses, discouraging us from taking part-time jobs. I have never had to work significant hours to survive or shoulder major family expenses. I am acutely aware that this is a privilege.

This privilege allowed me to accelerate. I completed my B.S. at UC Santa Cruz and my M.S. at UC Santa Barbara in a combined three and a half years. But as I moved through these programs, I met classmates whose high schools did not offer the same AP or honors courses mine had. They were motivated, but they faced material my high school had drilled for years. Seeing how uneven preparation could be made me feel responsible. I realized that since I had the luxury of time and focus, I should use my training to build tools that support those whose paths have been steeper.

This awareness shapes the projects I choose. I look for gaps where technology has failed to account for human variability or disability.

In Dr. Michael Beyeler’s Bionic Vision class, I encountered such a gap. Retinal implants can restore a form of vision to the blind, but the signal is often distorted, making reading standard text nearly impossible. I wondered: instead of waiting for better hardware, could we change the input? I developed a project called SymbolSight. The goal was to find visual symbols that remain distinct even when distorted by a prosthetic. I didn't just guess; I simulated the distortions using the pulse2percept framework and used a MobileNetV3 neural network to estimate confusion probabilities. I found that by mixing symbols from diverse sources—like Katakana, Braille, and DCT—we could create a "font" that was far more legible through an implant than standard Latin letters. It was a technical challenge, but the motivation was accessibility: trying to restore the fundamental right to read.

I saw a similar need for transparency in another context. I have watched non-native speaking relatives struggle with healthcare instructions and legal contracts. They would sign documents they didn't fully understand because the "rules of the game" were opaque to them. In Dr. Misha Sra’s course, I built a prototype called GUARD (Guided Understanding & Agreement Rights Detector). The system listens during high-stakes conversations—like a landlord explaining a lease or a doctor giving a diagnosis—and uses AI to highlight inconsistencies or likely problems. I designed a three-tab interface (Overview, Discuss, Resolve) to progressively disclose information so the user isn't overwhelmed. The tool doesn't make decisions for them; it offers them the agency to ask the right questions before they commit.

Even in lighter contexts, I try to build tools that reduce friction and anxiety. At SBHacks 2025, I thought back to the stress of managing deadlines in college. I built "SnipDue," a tool that lets students take a screenshot of their syllabus and automatically syncs the deadlines to their calendar using the Claude 3.5 Sonnet LLM. It was a technical success—winning "Best Use of Gen AI"—but for me, it was about lowering the cognitive load for students who are likely just as overwhelmed as I once was.

Mentorship is how I try to pass this perspective on. For the past two years, I have given guest lectures in Dr. Daniel Shapiro’s Game AI class at UCSC. I talk candidly about my project, AIPIF, and how I navigated the logistics of research. I don’t present myself as a prodigy. I walk through the messy intermediate steps. I tell them about the rejections, the bugs, and the uncertainty. I try to be the person I needed when I was in their position: someone who says, concretely, "Here is how you do this, and here is why you belong here."

Outside of academics, I look for hands-on work where I start as a beginner again. This summer, I began volunteering at the Silicon Valley Bicycle Exchange, a shop that repairs donated bikes for people who need transportation. When I started, I could barely fix a flat tire. Over months, I learned to tune derailleurs and true wheels. Now, I help fill trucks with commuter bikes I’ve restored. The riders will never know my name, but they will feel the brakes working and the gears shifting smoothly. That kind of behind-the-scenes contribution feels similar to good systems engineering: most people never see the code, but they experience the difference every time they use it.

These are small actions. I do not want to overstate them. But they reflect something I believe deeply: that access to knowledge and tools is unevenly distributed, and those of us who have received support have a responsibility to pass it on.

I do not fit traditional markers of "disadvantage," but my experiences with imposter syndrome in hyper-competitive environments have shaped how I move through computer science. They have taught me to pay attention to who is left out of our systems, and to see my technical work as part of a broader responsibility to make AI safer and more accessible.

If I am admitted, I will be the first person in my family to earn a PhD. I will bring experience in building interactive systems for non-experts, an awareness of the structural advantages that shaped my own education, and a record of choosing projects that support people left out of standard designs. As a PhD student in Computer Science at UC Santa Barbara, I hope to keep building tools that make complex systems more transparent, and to mentor students who do not see themselves as "natural" computer scientists. I want to help build a graduate community where high standards and sustainable, inclusive ways of working can coexist.

I am ready to move from questioning my place in this field to leading within it. I bring resilience born from early struggles, a proven track record of research, and a clear vision of how my work can serve the broader community.
