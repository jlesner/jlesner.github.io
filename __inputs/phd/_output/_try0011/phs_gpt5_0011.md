On a Saturday morning at the Silicon Valley Bicycle Exchange, I was hunched over a tiny pink bike, trying to get the rear brake pads to stop rubbing. A volunteer joked that my “patient” was probably late for third grade. I would never meet the rider, but I knew the bike might be their only reliable way to get to work or school. When we finally rolled it to the donation truck, I felt the quiet satisfaction of doing something small and technical so someone else’s path could be a little smoother.

That feeling has followed me through games and research. I grew up in Palo Alto, where our living room was permanently set up for LAN parties. My parents, both first-generation college graduates from Canada, did not limit “screen time”; they treated games as puzzles and practice.

High school at Henry M. Gunn added a different lesson. Gunn is known for its rigor, but during my time there it was also known for student suicide clusters, and I still remember adults standing guard by the Caltrain tracks after school. Achievement was everywhere, but vulnerability was mostly private. I did well on paper—a 3.9 GPA, plenty of APs—but I never felt like the “genius kid.” When I was admitted to only one UC campus, UC Santa Cruz, and only off the waitlist, it felt like confirmation that I was not the kind of person who went on to do a PhD. 

In college, that doubt collided with a clearer view of my own advantages. At Santa Cruz I met classmates whose high schools had offered far fewer advanced classes than mine. They were motivated, but they hit core CS courses with less preparation and sometimes failed the same class more than once. Meanwhile, my parents insisted that my sister and I not work part-time so we could focus on school; they helped with tuition and rent. I have never had to send money home or choose between homework and a job shift. Seeing how much easier my path had been, I stopped treating grades as neutral measures of talent and started thinking about access and uneven preparation.

That awareness now shapes the projects I choose. At SBHacks 2025, my team built SnipDue, a web app that lets students paste messy course schedules into a page and turn them into calendar events they can drag, edit, and sync. Under the hood, Cloudflare Workers call an LLM to parse deadlines, but the interface keeps students in control by “thinking aloud,” marking ambiguities, and letting them override the model before anything touches their calendars.  In a graduate bionic vision course at UC Santa Barbara, I worked on SymbolSight, a pipeline that draws symbols from diverse writing systems, simulates how they would appear through retinal prostheses, and optimizes mappings between letters and symbols so that text stays as legible as possible under severe distortions.  And in Professor Misha Sra’s class, I designed GUARD, a phone-based assistant that listens alongside users during high-stakes conversations, compares spoken explanations to written terms, and uses a progressively disclosed three-tab interface to flag inconsistencies without overwhelming people on small screens.  Across very different domains, these projects share a goal: using AI to reduce cognitive load and make complex information safer to navigate for people who may not have time, training, or language advantages.

Research has not been a straight climb. When I first applied to graduate school, I chose a master’s program rather than a PhD because I was afraid I could not sustain several years of research. During my M.S. at UC Santa Barbara I learned that I could: I completed my degree quickly, published work on interactive fiction for children, and discovered that what I enjoy most is the slow, iterative process of turning messy ideas into tested systems. 

For the past two years, my former advisor at UC Santa Cruz has invited me back to guest lecture in his Game AI class. Many students there remind me of how I felt as an undergraduate—interested in AI but unsure if they are “research material.” Instead of presenting myself as an expert, I walk them through the concrete steps we took on a project that eventually became a paper and talk frankly about dead ends, prompt failures, and scope creep. We also talk about mental health and sustainable workloads, drawing a line between the crunch culture of the game industry and the habits they are forming now. 

Outside the lab and classroom, places like the bicycle exchange keep me grounded. When I started volunteering, I could barely fix my own flat tire. Now I can take a rusted, unrideable frame and, with guidance, turn it into something safe enough for someone to rely on every day.

I do not come from a background that fits traditional checkboxes of disadvantage. My parents’ support has given me time and stability that many of my peers have not had. At the same time, growing up in a high-pressure academic environment, struggling with imposter syndrome, and watching the costs of neglecting mental health have shaped how I move through computer science. I have learned to pay attention to who is quietly struggling, to design tools that respect users’ limits and constraints, and to treat mentorship as part of my responsibility, not an optional extra. 

As a PhD student in computer science, I hope to keep following that pattern: notice gaps, build systems that close them, and share what I learn so others can do the same. I want to develop human-centered AI tools that make complex systems more transparent and usable for people who have less preparation, less free time, or less institutional power—from first-generation college students managing chaotic schedules to patients navigating opaque medical advice. I also hope to help foster a research culture where high standards and humane expectations coexist. In that sense, the bike shop, the classroom, and the lab all point in the same direction: careful, collaborative work that makes someone else’s ride a little safer and more predictable, even if they never learn my name.
