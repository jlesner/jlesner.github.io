# Personal History Statement

Our living room looked like a small data center. My dad had run Ethernet cables along the baseboards so my sister and I could play networked games without lag. It was a Saturday afternoon, and we were deep into a cooperative puzzle game—the kind where you have to talk constantly to coordinate moves. My mom brought in snacks and stayed to watch. She didn't understand the mechanics, but she understood what was happening: her kids were learning to communicate under pressure, to fail and retry, to break a hard problem into smaller pieces. My parents—both first-generation college graduates who had put themselves through Canadian universities—never saw games as distractions. They saw them as practice.

That early trust in my curiosity shaped how I think about technology today. I stopped wanting to just play games and started wanting to know how they worked. I read wikis about game engines, watched programming tutorials, and eventually joined online game jams where you get a weekend and a strange constraint—"only one button," "everything is a circle"—and have to build something playable by strangers. Those jams led to hackathons in college. The format was similar, but the targets shifted: instead of entertaining players, I was building tools under real constraints. Through those experiences, I realized what drew me in wasn't games alone. It was computer science, and the craft of making software that helps people.

My parents' background made this path easier than it is for many students. They wanted my sister and me to focus fully on school. They helped with tuition and living expenses and discouraged us from taking part-time jobs. I have never had to work significant hours to support myself or my family. I know this is a privilege.

---

But privilege doesn't immunize you from doubt. I grew up in Palo Alto and attended Gunn High School, one of the most academically competitive public schools in the country. Many of my classmates had been doing competition math since middle school. I had not. When I struggled with a concept, I assumed the problem was me—that I lacked some essential aptitude others possessed. I finished high school with a 3.9 GPA, but I didn't trust it. When I applied to several UCs, I was admitted only to Santa Cruz, and only off the waitlist. At the time, this felt like confirmation.

Gunn added another layer. During my years there, the community experienced suicide clusters among students. I remember adults standing guard at the Caltrain tracks near campus. It left a deep impression. I became wary of cultures that celebrate achievement while ignoring mental health. Video games, for me and my classmates, became a low-stakes space to decompress and stay connected—teaming up in an online match or just talking over a casual game. Those experiences are part of why, as a college student, I check in on peers who seem overwhelmed. I talk openly about scoping work. I treat sustainable pace as just as important as grades or publications.

I carried my doubt into my undergraduate years. I worked hard, but I hedged. When I first applied to graduate school, I chose a master's program rather than a PhD because I wasn't confident I could sustain five or six years of research. I thought I might fail.

I didn't.

---

At UC Santa Cruz, I met classmates whose high schools hadn't offered the same AP or honors courses mine did. They were motivated, but when they reached core CS courses, they faced material my high school had drilled for years. Some failed classes more than once and left the major entirely. Seeing how uneven preparation can be changed how I think about GPAs—I don't treat them as neutral indicators of talent. It made me feel responsible for using my training to build tools that support people whose paths have been steeper than mine.

This awareness shapes the projects I choose. At a hackathon, my team built SnipDue, a web app that lets students paste messy course schedules and sync cleaned-up deadlines to their calendars. It won "Best Use of Gen AI" at SBHacks 2025. The idea came from watching classmates miss assignments because manually entering deadlines is tedious and error-prone. The tool gives humans final say over what the AI suggests—a small example of human-AI collaboration done carefully.

In Dr. Misha Sra's class, I prototyped GUARD, a phone-based assistant that cross-checks documents and listens during high-stakes conversations—like a landlord explaining a lease or a doctor making recommendations—and highlights likely problems. The idea came from watching non-native-speaking relatives struggle with healthcare instructions and sign contracts they didn't fully understand. In those situations, the rules of the game are opaque to anyone without legal or medical fluency. GUARD offers another chance to notice problems before committing to terms.

For Dr. Michael Beyeler's bionic vision course, I developed SymbolSight, a system that selects visual symbols designed to remain distinguishable even under the distortions caused by retinal implants. The project was voted best in the class, and we're now preparing a paper for IEEE EMBC 2026. The work is preliminary—simulations, not user studies—but it points toward restoring reading ability for people who have lost it.

---

Mentorship has become another way I try to pass on what I've received. For two years, I've given guest lectures in Dr. Daniel Shapiro's Game AI class at UC Santa Cruz. I talk with undergraduates about scoping feasible projects, choosing teammates, and planning around the unreliability of AI tools. I don't present myself as a prodigy. I walk through messy intermediate steps and give practical advice. Many of these students remind me of myself—curious but uncertain, interested but unsure if they belong. I try to be the person I needed: someone who says, concretely, here is how you do this, and here is why you can.

Outside academics, I volunteer weekly at Silicon Valley Bicycle Exchange, a community shop that repairs donated bikes for people who need transportation to work or school. When I started, I could barely fix a flat. Now I help fill trucks with kids' and commuter bikes I helped restore. The riders won't know my name, but they'll feel working brakes and wheels that don't rub. That kind of behind-the-scenes contribution feels similar to good software: most people never see the code, but they experience the difference.

---

If admitted, I will be the first person in my family to earn a PhD. I bring experience building interactive systems for children and non-experts, awareness of the structural advantages that shaped my education, and a habit of choosing projects that support people left out of standard designs. I am ready to move from questioning my place in this field to contributing within it—building tools that make complex systems more transparent and mentoring students who, like me, once doubted they belonged.
