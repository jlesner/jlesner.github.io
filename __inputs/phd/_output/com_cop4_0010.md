## **Statement of Purpose**

I aim to develop methods that make large language models more reliable and trustworthy—specifically through automatic prompt optimization and mechanistic interpretability. My interest in this direction grew from direct experience: building LLM-powered systems that worked brilliantly in demos but failed unpredictably in deployment. I want to understand why, and I want to fix it.

My academic preparation spans systems, machine learning, and formal methods. At UC Santa Cruz, I completed coursework in algorithms, operating systems, and game AI. At UCSB, I took graduate seminars in foundation models, neural information retrieval, and bionic vision. Beyond coursework, I have strong implementation skills in Python, XPATH/XSLT for structured transformations, and experience deploying models using vLLM on GPU clusters. I have worked with SMT solvers, graph neural networks, and multi-agent LLM pipelines. This combination—systems thinking plus ML foundations plus formal reasoning—shapes how I approach research problems.

My first sustained research experience was the State Machine Visualizer project, which I began as an undergraduate research assistant under Dr. Gabriel Elkaim at UCSC. The goal was to automatically generate visual diagrams of state machines from C source code, helping mechatronics students debug complex robot control logic. I designed an annotation pipeline using Abstract Syntax Tree analysis with XPATH/XSLT rule sets to identify state variables, transitions, and guard conditions. The hardest part was extending the system to handle the diversity of student implementations—I collected code samples across two academic years to ensure robustness. When early regex-based approaches failed, I backtracked and rebuilt using proper parsing. The tool is now used in UCSC's mechatronics curriculum, and I am preparing a paper for publication. This project taught me that research requires persistence through dead ends.

During the gap between my bachelor's and master's degrees, I developed AIPIF (AI Personalized Interactive Fiction), a system that lets young children create interactive stories using generative AI while giving parents oversight over content. I designed a "tree of thought" approach where the LLM develops parallel narrative branches, then used XSLT to transform the story structure into playable web-based fiction. The system integrates text generation, image synthesis, and music generation. I wrote this work up independently with mentorship from Dr. Daniel Shapiro, and to my surprise, both PAIS 2024 and ECAI 2024 accepted my papers. Presenting at ECAI in Santiago de Compostela—my first international conference—was formative. I spoke too fast, I could have been more inquisitive about others' work, but I left knowing I wanted to keep doing this.

At UCSB, my research has focused on LLM reliability in formal and semi-formal domains. In the MIRROR project for Professor Tao Yang's neural information retrieval course, I replicated a SIGIR 2024 paper on LLM-based document re-ranking. I uncovered a 96% discrepancy in efficiency metrics due to under-specified batch sizes—a reminder that reproducibility requires obsessive attention to implementation details. My prompt engineering experiments yielded up to 40% improvements in ranking accuracy, demonstrating how sensitive these systems are to exact wording.

My most substantial recent work is DBDoctor, a system that uses LLMs to extend the coverage of SMT-based SQL equivalence verifiers. Many formal verification tools cannot handle modern SQL features like window functions. DBDoctor uses an LLM to rewrite complex queries into verifier-compatible forms, then validates any discovered counterexamples against the original queries to filter spurious results. I evaluated the system on nearly 24,000 LeetCode query pairs, demonstrating extended coverage and discovery of new counterexamples. This work, supervised by Professor Xifeng Yan with Dr. Fuheng Zhao, is being prepared for CAV 2026.

My master's project, HAPO (Hyper-Reflection for Automatic Prompt Optimization), investigated how to optimize prompts for compact models on verifiable tasks. Using the GEPA framework, I optimized prompts for SQL synthesis and analysis, achieving 100% success on synthesis and 75% accuracy on counterexample discovery—outperforming baselines by 30%. I introduced "Hyper-Reflection," a meta-optimization technique that uses frontier models to improve GEPA's reflection mechanism itself. I also identified and resolved a critical failure mode: sophisticated reflection prompts caused compact models to exhaust their generation budget on chain-of-thought reasoning before producing answers. Extending context windows via YaRN solved this. The project crystallized my interest in automatic prompt optimization as a research direction.

I want to continue my PhD with Professor Xifeng Yan, building on the collaboration we developed during my master's year. Dr. Yan's lab works on foundation models, multi-agent systems, and the ADL/MICA framework for declarative agent specification. My interest in prompt optimization complements this agenda: where ADL/MICA specifies agent behavior in YAML, my work could automatically optimize those specifications. Dr. Yan's Directional Stimulus Prompting research trains small policy models to guide larger LLMs—my HAPO and future LEAP work extends this from stimulus generation to full prompt rewriting. I see a natural fit between my technical interests and the lab's research trajectory.

More broadly, I am drawn to UCSB because of its culture of rigorous, collaborative research. I have already spent a year learning the department's rhythms—attending seminars, working with faculty across courses, and engaging with the graduate community. I know I can thrive here.

After my PhD, I want to work in industry research, building AI systems that are robust enough for high-stakes deployment. The specific domain I care most about is oncology—cancer killed both my grandfather and my uncle, and as a blood relative, this is not abstract to me. But I believe the bottleneck in medical AI is not disease-specific models; it is that current systems are brittle and opaque. By working on foundational questions of LLM reliability now, I will be better positioned to build systems that clinicians can actually trust.

I am ready for this work. I have published, presented internationally, and spent a year proving I can do graduate research at UCSB. I want to keep going.

## **Personal History Statement**

I was sixteen, sitting in a pre-calculus class at Gunn High School in Palo Alto, and I could not follow the lecture. The teacher moved quickly. The students around me nodded along. I stared at my notebook and felt certain I did not belong.

Gunn is one of the most academically competitive public high schools in the country. Many of my classmates had been doing competition math since middle school. I had not. When I struggled with a concept, I assumed the problem was me—that I lacked some essential aptitude that others possessed. I finished high school with a 3.9 GPA, but I did not trust it. When I applied to several UCs, I was admitted only to Santa Cruz, and only off the waitlist. At the time, this felt like confirmation.

I carried that doubt into my undergraduate years. I worked hard, but I hedged. When I first applied to graduate school, I chose a master's program rather than a PhD because I was not confident I could sustain five or six years of research. I thought I might fail.

Completing my master's at UCSB in one year changed that.

The experience showed me that I can do graduate-level work—not just survive it, but thrive in it. I took demanding courses, published research, presented at an international conference, and left feeling that my time had been too short rather than too long. The self-doubt I carried from high school began to feel less like truth and more like a story I had told myself for too long.

Looking back, I see a pattern. I notice gaps—between my confidence and my ability, between who I thought I was and what I could actually do—and I slowly close them. This pattern extends beyond academics.

My family has been shaped by cancer. My grandfather died from it. My uncle died from it. As a blood relative, I know the statistics apply to me, to my mother, to my sister. This is not an abstraction. It is a reason to care about whether the AI systems we build actually work—whether they are robust, reliable, and trustworthy enough to inform real medical decisions. I cannot build those systems yet. But I can work on the foundational problems that will make them possible, and I can do so with an urgency that comes from knowing what is at stake.

I have also tried to address gaps I see in my community. For two years, I have given guest lectures to Dr. Daniel Shapiro's Game AI class at UCSC, talking with undergraduates about how to get started with LLM-based projects and research. Many of them remind me of myself—curious but uncertain, interested but unsure if they belong. I try to be the person I needed when I was in their position: someone who says, concretely, here is how you do this, and here is why you can.

At UCSB, I have been involved with the Women in Computer Science (WiCS) club, talking with undergraduates about graduate school and research. Many of them are first-generation students or come from backgrounds underrepresented in CS. I do not assume they lack ability; I assume they may lack information, or mentorship, or the simple experience of someone telling them they can do this. I try to provide that.

These are small actions. I do not want to overstate them. But they reflect something I believe: that access to knowledge and encouragement is unevenly distributed, and that those of us who have received it have some responsibility to pass it on.

If I am admitted to UCSB's PhD program, I will continue this work. I will mentor undergraduates, particularly those from backgrounds underrepresented in AI research. I will be honest about the challenges I faced—the self-doubt, the gaps in my preparation, the slow process of learning to trust my own abilities—because I think that honesty is more useful than pretending the path was easy.

I also hope to bring a perspective shaped by my family's experience with cancer. When I work on AI reliability, I will not forget why it matters. Systems that fail unpredictably are not just scientifically unsatisfying; they are dangerous when deployed in medicine, in infrastructure, in any domain where errors have human costs. I want to build systems that work, and I want to do so with the seriousness that high-stakes applications demand.

I am not the same person who sat in that pre-calculus class, certain I did not belong. I have learned that belonging is something you build through work, through persistence, through showing up even when you doubt yourself. I have built it at UCSC, and I have built it at UCSB. I am ready to continue.
