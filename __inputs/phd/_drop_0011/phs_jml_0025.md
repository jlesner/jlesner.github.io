


## Personal History Statement – UCSB Computer Science

I grew up in Palo Alto with a childhood immered in around video games. While many of my friends had strict “screen time” limits, my parents—both first-generation college graduates—did something unusual: they encouraged my sister and me to play. Our living room was always set up for “LAN parties” because to them, games were not a distraction but a place to practice problem solving, persistence, and collaboration. They saw games as educational tools, not temptations to be removed. That early trust in my curiosity shaped how I see technology today: as something to be explored, tinkered with, and to help people who struggle or are left out.

As I got older, I stopped being satisfied just playing games and started wondering how they worked. I spent afternoons reading wikis about game engines, watching YouTube game programming tutorials, and hacking up prototypes in tools like Unity. I joined online game jams where you have a weekend and a theme—“only one button,” “everything is a circle”—and have to design something that is fun, understandable, and playable by strangers. Those jams led naturally to hackathons once I reached college. The format was similar, but the stakes and targets were different: instead of entertaining players, we were building tools under time and resource constraints. Through those experiences I realized that what drew me in was not games alone but computer science more broadly and the craft of making software.

My parents’ background made my path into CS easier than it is for many students. They had both put themselves through Canadian universities and wanted my sister and me to focus fully on school. They helped with tuition and living expenses and discouraged us from taking part-time jobs. I have never had to work significant hours or shoulder major family expenses, and I am very aware that this is a privilege. 

----


Growing up in Palo Alto, I attended Gunn High School, a place known for its intense academic pressure. While my peers seemed to effortlessly grasp advanced calculus, I struggled with math. I internalized a narrative that I was behind, that I lacked the innate "spark" required for deep technical work. When I applied to colleges, I was rejected by most of the UC system and admitted to UC Santa Cruz only off the waitlist. I carried that sense of imposter syndrome with me for years.

At the same time, being surrounded by genious kids in I struggled with feeling inferror. 

High school at Henry M. Gunn in Palo Alto added another layer of complexity. Gunn was a high-pressure environment with a strong reputation for academic rigor, but during my time there the community experienced suicide clusters among students. Watching adults stand guard at the Caltrain tracks near campus left a deep impression on me. It made me wary of cultures that celebrate achievement while ignoring mental health. Video games, for me and classmates, became a low-stakes space to decompress and stay connected, whether that meant teaming up in an online match or just talking over a casual game. Those experiences are part of why, as a college student, I check in on peers who seem overwhelmed, to talk openly about scoping work, and to treat sustainable workloads as just as important as grades or publications.

Gunn is one of the most academically competitive public high schools in the country. Many of my classmates had been doing competition math since middle school. I had not. When I struggled with a concept, I assumed the problem was me—that I lacked some essential aptitude that others possessed. I finished high school with a 3.9 GPA, but I did not trust it. When I applied to several UCs, I was admitted only to Santa Cruz, and only off the waitlist. At the time, this felt like confirmation.





At the same time, I grew up in Palo Alto and attended Gunn High School, a place where it sometimes felt like everyone else already had a roadmap to Stanford, research internships, and startup ideas. I did well academically, but I internalized a quiet assumption that I wasn’t the kind of person who goes on to do a PhD. No one in my immediate family worked in academia; the people I knew who did cutting-edge work were always someone else’s parents. 




---

I carried that doubt into my undergraduate years. I worked hard, but I hedged. When I first applied to graduate school, I chose a master's program rather than a PhD because I was not confident I could sustain five or six years of research. I thought I might fail.

However, the hesitation lingered. After graduating, I chose a Master’s degree at UCSB rather than a PhD because I was afraid to commit. I needed to know if I could handle the rigor.


Looking back, I see a pattern. I notice gaps—between my confidence and my ability, between who I thought I was and what I could actually do—and I slowly close them. This pattern extends beyond academics.

I completed my B.S. at UC Santa Cruz and my M.S. at UC Santa Barbara in a combined three and a half years, again made possible by not needing part-time jobs. At UCSC I met classmates whose high schools did not offer the same range of AP or honors courses that mine did. They were motivated, but when they reached core CS courses, they faced material my own high school had drilled for years. Some failed classes more than once, and left the major entirely. Having grown up in a high-income town and having had time to focus on academics, I do not treat GPAs as neutral indicators of talent. Seeing how uneven preparation can be made me feel responsible for using my training to build tools to support those whose paths have been steeper than mine.


This awareness shapes the projects I choose.

In another project, for Dr. Mrsa Sra I prototyped a phone-based assistant called GUARD which cross checks documents, emails and listens during high stakes conversations—such as a landlord explaining a lease or a doctor making recommendations  — and uses AI to highlight likely problems and suggest follow-up questions. The idea came from watching my non native speaking relatives struggle with healthcare instructions and sign contracts they did not fully understand and realizing that in those situations, the “rules of the game” are opaque to anyone without medical or legal fluency. GUARD offers another chance to notice problems and inconsistencies and ask questions before committing to terms.

Mentorship and community have become the ways I try to pass that empathy on. For the past two years, I have given guest lectures in Dr. Shapiro’s Game AI class, talking candidly about a course project called AIPIF and how I navigated the logistics of writing and submitting papers. I don’t present myself as a prodigy; instead, I walk through the messy intermediate steps and try to give practical advice.

My AI Personalized Interactive Fiction (AIPIF) project began in UCSC’s “Game AI” course, where I set out to build story-based games for small children that adapt to their interests and choices. That project later became a paper and demo at PAIS-24 and ECAI-24. My advisor, Dr. Shapiro, now invites me back as a guest lecturer to mentor his students. Since my published paper is on the reading list, I focus on helping his students scope feasible projects, choose complementary teammates, and plan around the unreliability of modern AI tools. We also talk about avoiding last-minute “crunch” that resembles unhealthy game-industry work cycles. I try to demystify research by showing that with persistence and careful planning even undergraduates can ship something real -- whether that is a paper, a tool, or a game.

For two years, I have given guest lectures to Dr. Daniel Shapiro's Game AI class at UCSC, talking with undergraduates about how to get started with LLM-based projects and research. Many of them remind me of myself—curious but uncertain, interested but unsure if they belong. I try to be the person I needed when I was in their position: someone who says, concretely, here is how you do this, and here is why you can.

---


Outside of academics, I look for hands-on work where I start as a beginner again and the impact is practical and quiet. This summer I began weekly volunteer shifts at Silicon Valley Bicycle Exchange, a community bike shop that repairs donated bikes for people who need transportation to work or school. When I started, I could barely fix my own flat tire. Over a few months I learned basic repairs and safety checks. Now I help fill the backs of trucks with kids’ and commuter bikes I helped restore. The riders will not know my name, but they will feel working brakes and wheels that do not rub. That kind of behind-the-scenes contribution feels similar to good level design in a game: most people never see the code or state diagrams, but they experience the difference every time they ride.

---

These are small actions. I do not want to overstate them. But they reflect something I believe: that access to knowledge and encouragement is unevenly distributed, and that those of us who have received it have some responsibility to pass it on.
