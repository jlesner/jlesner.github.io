I grew up in Palo Alto with a childhood organized around video games. While many of my friends had strict “screen time” limits, my parents—both first-generation college graduates—did something unusual: they encouraged my sister and me to play. Our living room was always set up for “LAN parties” because to them, games were not a distraction but a place to practice problem solving, persistence, and collaboration. They saw games as educational tools, not temptations to be removed. That early trust in my curiosity shaped how I see technology today: as something to be explored, tinkered with, and to help people who struggle or are left out.

As I got older, I stopped being satisfied just playing games and started wondering how they worked. I spent afternoons reading wikis about game engines, watching YouTube game programming tutorials, and hacking up prototypes in tools like Unity. I joined online game jams where you have a weekend and a theme—“only one button,” “everything is a circle”—and have to design something that is fun, understandable, and playable by strangers. Those jams led naturally to hackathons once I reached college. The format was similar, but the stakes and targets were different: instead of entertaining players, we were building tools under time and resource constraints. Through those experiences I realized that what drew me in was not games alone but computer science more broadly and the craft of making software.

My parents’ background made my path into CS easier than it is for many students. They had both put themselves through Canadian universities and wanted my sister and me to focus fully on school. They helped with tuition and living expenses and discouraged us from taking part-time jobs. I have never had to work significant hours or shoulder major family expenses, and I am very aware that this is a privilege. At the same time, I struggled with math in a way that did not fit the stereotype of a “natural” programmer. I spent summers in remedial math classes while friends traveled. Those summers felt frustrating, but they taught me that my way forward depended on repetition, structure, and patience—skills I later saw echoed in debugging code and iterating on software prototypes.

High school at Henry M. Gunn in Palo Alto added another layer of complexity. Gunn was a high-pressure environment with a strong reputation for academic rigor, but during my time there the community experienced suicide clusters among students. Watching adults stand guard at the Caltrain tracks near campus left a deep impression on me. It made me wary of cultures that celebrate achievement while ignoring mental health. Video games, for me and classmates, became a low-stakes space to decompress and stay connected, whether that meant teaming up in an online match or just talking over a casual game. Those experiences are part of why, as a college student, I check in on peers who seem overwhelmed, to talk openly about scoping work, and to treat sustainable workloads as just as important as grades or publications.

I completed my B.S. at UC Santa Cruz and my M.S. at UC Santa Barbara in a combined three and a half years, again made possible by not needing part-time jobs. At UCSC I met classmates whose high schools did not offer the same range of AP or honors courses that mine did. They were motivated, but when they reached core CS courses, they faced material my own high school had drilled for years. Some failed classes more than once, and left the major entirely. Having grown up in a high-income town and having had time to focus on academics, I do not treat GPAs as neutral indicators of talent. Seeing how uneven preparation can be made me feel responsible for using my training to build tools to support those whose paths have been steeper than mine.

This awareness shapes the projects I choose. In NutriGNN, I worked with USDA food composition data and noticed that many entries were missing, especially for foods common in low-income or culturally specific diets. We used graph-based models to estimate those missing values so that nutrition studies can include the full range of foods people actually eat, not just those that are best documented. Game development taught me to think in terms of feedback loops and edge cases; NutriGNN extended that mindset to public health, asking whose data is missing from the “game state” and what that does to the outcomes researchers model.

In another project, for Dr. Mrsa Sra I prototyped a phone-based assistant called GUARD which cross checks documents, emails and listens during high stakes conversations—such as a landlord explaining a lease or a doctor making recommendations  — and uses AI to highlight likely problems and suggest follow-up questions. The idea came from watching my non native speaking relatives struggle with healthcare instructions and sign contracts they did not fully understand and realizing that in those situations, the “rules of the game” are opaque to anyone without medical or legal fluency. GUARD offers another chance to notice problems and inconsistencies and ask questions before committing to terms.

My AI Personalized Interactive Fiction (AIPIF) project began in UCSC’s “Game AI” course, where I set out to build story-based games for small children that adapt to their interests and choices. That project later became a paper and demo at PAIS-24 and ECAI-24. My advisor, Dr. Shapiro, now invites me back as a guest lecturer to mentor his students. Since my published paper is on the reading list, I focus on helping his students scope feasible projects, choose complementary teammates, and plan around the unreliability of modern AI tools. We also talk about avoiding last-minute “crunch” that resembles unhealthy game-industry work cycles. I try to demystify research, especially for students from underrepresented backgrounds, by showing that with persistence and careful planning they can ship something real—whether that is a paper, a tool, or a game.

Outside of academics, I look for hands-on work where I start as a beginner again and the impact is practical and quiet. Recently I began weekly volunteer shifts at Silicon Valley Bicycle Exchange, a community bike shop that repairs donated bikes for people who need transportation to work or school. When I started, I could barely fix my own flat tire. Over a few months I learned basic repairs and safety checks. Now I help fill the back of a truck with kids’ and commuter bikes I helped restore. The riders will not know my name, but they will feel working brakes and wheels that do not rub. That kind of behind-the-scenes contribution feels similar to good level design in a game: most people never see the code or state diagrams, but they experience the difference every time they ride.

Taken together, my path—from a childhood spent gaming with my parents’ encouragement, to online game jams and college hackathons, to research and community service—has given me a perspective that blends playful experimentation with a serious commitment to equity and mental health. If I am admitted, I will be the first person in my family to earn a PhD. I will bring experience in building interactive systems for children and non-experts, awareness of the structural advantages that shaped my own education, and a record of choosing projects that support people left out of standard designs. As a PhD student in Computer Science at UC Santa Barbara, I hope to keep building tools that make complex systems more transparent and fair, and to mentor students who do not see themselves as “natural” computer scientists, so that high standards, inclusive design, and sustainable ways of working can coexist in the graduate community.
